"Judged by human experts": how often finance professionals agree on whether an AI answer is right

By Sandeep Singh Rai, Founder

When a bank or its vendor says an AI assistant "answers finance questions correctly X% of the time", someone, somewhere, marked the answers. Kensho, the AI company owned by S&P Global, published one of the most careful versions of that exercise in "No Free Labels" (Krumdick et al.), whereby for its 160 business and finance questions (BFF-Bench), financial professionals wrote the questions and reference answers, six AI models answered them, and each of the 960 finance answers was marked three times from a pool of nine financial analysts, as Correct, Incorrect or Not sure, with the marks then used to test AI judges. In August 2026 Kensho released all three marks for every answer under an Apache 2.0 licence, which is exactly what makes a check like this possible. The credit for that is theirs, as it is for stating in the paper that answers without a consensus were set aside as ambiguous.

What we found

1. The published figures reproduce. Every count in the paper's Table 1 and all twelve accuracy figures in Table 2 come out of the released marks exactly, as do the 1,036 answers and 604 answer pairs the AI judges were tested on.

2. On finance, the three experts gave the same verdict on fewer than half the answers. All three said Correct, or all three said Incorrect, on 459 of 960 answers: 48% (95% interval 44% to 52%). On the maths and reasoning answers, which the paper's authors marked themselves, the figure is 74%.

One expert mark in six on finance was "Not sure" (16.6%). On the maths and reasoning answers, marked by the paper's authors, it was one in fifteen (6.5%).

3. One finance answer in six has no expert verdict, and every judge figure is measured on the rest. For 152 of the 960 finance answers (15.8%, 95% interval 13% to 19%), no two experts gave the same verdict of Correct or Incorrect. The paper sets these aside, as it says, so AI judges are scored on the other 808. Of those 808, 349 (43%) rest on two experts out of three. In the paper's head-to-head test, where a judge picks the better of two answers, both answers were marked unanimously in 131 of the 442 finance pairs (30%).

4. The published accuracy treats an answer with no verdict as not correct. The paper's best finance result is GPT-4o at 68.1% correct. That is the share of GPT-4o's 160 finance answers that a majority of experts marked Correct. A majority marked 17.5% Incorrect, and the other 14.4% have no verdict. So the share of GPT-4o's finance answers that were wrong, by the experts' majority, lies between 17.5% and 31.9%, depending on how the unsettled answers are counted. On the answers the experts did settle, GPT-4o was right 79.6% of the time. The same split applies to every model:

5. With margins, the finance ranking has one clear leader and two ties. Each published accuracy carries about plus or minus 8 points. GPT-4o is clearly ahead, 16 points above Phi-4 (95% interval 8 to 25). Phi-4, Llama 3.3 70B and Qwen 2.5 7B cannot be told apart (gaps of 0 to 6 points, every interval includes zero), and neither can Yi 1.5 34B and Gemma 2 2B (1 point, interval −7 to +9).

6. The paper's main finding holds, and the experts' own agreement shows how far a judge can be tested. The paper reports that an AI judge given the expert-written reference answer agrees with the experts far better than one without it: Cohen's kappa of 0.63 to 0.78 across five judges with the reference, against 0.24 to 0.47 without (Table 6, first prompt). For scale, two experts who both gave a verdict agree at kappa 0.78 on those same answers (0.74 to 0.82), and an expert checked against the other two, where they agree, reaches 0.86 (0.82 to 0.89). The best judge with a reference, Llama 3.3 70B at 0.78, comes close to the experts; these marks cannot show it matches them.

Intervals resample whole conversations, so they allow for each of the 80 finance conversations carrying twelve answers.

Why it matters

A bank validating an AI assistant on accounting and finance questions will, in all likelihood, do what Kensho did: have its own experts mark the answers, then check an automated judge against them. This is a careful, well-resourced version of that exercise, and it shows what to expect, being experts who are unanimous on about half the answers, an error rate that moves by 14 points for the best model, and a judge that can only be shown to be as good as the experts agree with each other. Three numbers, I believe, are worth asking for beside any accuracy figure that says "judged by experts": how often the experts agreed, how many answers got no verdict and how they were counted, and the margin on the figure.
