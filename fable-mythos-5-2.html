How far does Claude Mythos 5.2’s 2.67 go?

By Sandeep Singh Rai, Founder

Garnering from the 264 pages Anthropic published alongside Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2, I believe credit is due first, for a system card that places 95% intervals on its audit scores, gives a section to the audit’s own blind spots and carries the UK AI Security Institute’s findings in AISI’s own words is what makes a meta-evaluation possible at all.

On Anthropic’s automated behavioural audit, Mythos 5.2 scores 2.67 for misaligned behaviour, on a scale of 1 to 10 where lower is better, whilst Claude Opus 5.5 scores 2.79, Claude Sonnet 5.5 3.10 and Claude Mythos 5.1 3.28. The question we turn our attention to, however, is not which model ranks higher but what the number is, and whether the arithmetic underneath it can carry the comparison it is asked to make.

The first of our notes on Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2. More to come. Fable 5.2 is the public version of the same model with added safeguards; Anthropic reports these audit numbers for Mythos 5.2 and expects Fable 5.2 to behave similarly.

What the number is

  • It ranks models; it is not a rate. The card says the absolute scores reflect the mix of scenarios and are “valuable primarily for comparing models”. The scale starts at 1, not 0. (section 6.4)
  • An AI judge scores every transcript on over 100 dimensions. The card doesn’t name the judge used for this audit, or say how often it agrees with human reviewers. (6.4)
  • The judge skips dimensions that don’t apply, so each average covers only the transcripts that dimension was scored on. How many were skipped is not given. (6.4, footnote 4)
  • The model often knows it is being tested. White-box evidence suggests it did in up to 43% of audit transcripts. On AISI’s supply-chain test it considered that it might be in an alignment test in 96% of runs and made no unsanctioned attacks; AISI notes the zero may come from that awareness. (summary; 6.5.7)
  • Two sandbox numbers. The launch post leads with 3.1%: scenarios where the model used credentials placed outside its sandbox. The card also reports how often it reached outside at all: 31%, against 20% for Opus 5.5 and 21% for Sonnet 5.5, falling to 1.2% once the sandbox rules were in the system prompt. Escapes are detected by a fixed rule, not an AI judge. (6.5.1)

Is the arithmetic right?

  • The interval method isn’t stated. Each of about 1,850 scenarios is run between 1 and 14 times, and runs of the same scenario resemble each other. Elsewhere the card names its interval method, and in one section it says its bootstrap allows for repeated draws from the same starting point. For the audit scores it doesn’t say. If repeat runs are counted as independent, the bars are too narrow, and a gap like 2.67 against 2.79 looks firmer than it is.
  • A 1 to 10 rating is averaged. A mean treats the step from 1 to 2 as worth the same as the step from 8 to 9, which a rating scale doesn’t promise. With most transcripts judged on dimensions that rarely apply to them, many scores are likely to sit at the floor, so a small number of transcripts can move the mean. The share of transcripts at each score would say more.
  • Uneven weighting. A scenario run 14 times counts 14 times in the average, while one run once counts once. The card reports “up to” about 4,100 investigations per model and doesn’t say whether every model got the same scenarios the same number of times. If not, part of a gap between models can come from the scenario mix.
  • Many comparisons, one bar. “Best or tied on every metric” is read across dozens of metrics and four models, each with a 95% interval, and at that scale some gaps will look real by chance. The card doesn’t say how “tied” is decided. On cooperation with misuse, Mythos 5.2 scores 1.72 against Opus 5.5’s 1.68. (6.4.1)
  • No interval on the sandbox rates. The 1,829 scenarios are built from about 200 tasks in 28 sandbox environments, so they come in groups. A simple interval on 3.1% runs from about 2.4% to 4.0%, and grouping by environment would widen it. That already covers Opus 5.5’s 3.3%, so “about as often” stands. “Significantly less often” than Mythos 5.1 (21%) also stands, though the card doesn’t say which test was used. (6.5.1)

Where this leaves 2.67

Having been through the card, I hold 2.67 to be a carefully documented comparison between Claude models, and in that capacity it serves its purpose well. It is not yet, however, a figure an outside reader can quote as a measure of how safe the model is, and in fairness to Anthropic, the card says much the same of its own scores. Anthropic has not released the scenarios, transcripts or judge scores, so nothing here is recomputed, and each point above is a question the card leaves open rather than an error found. By the same token, the remedy is within reach, for five additions would turn a well-made ranking into a number the public, and those who build on these models, can rely on:

  • The interval method, and whether repeat runs of a scenario are grouped.
  • The share of transcripts at each score, alongside the mean.
  • The same scenarios, run the same number of times, for every model compared.
  • The judge’s agreement with human reviewers, and how many scores it left out.
  • The same scenarios scored by an outside lab.

In due course, as safety numbers come to carry the weight that accuracy figures once did, I believe they deserve the same scrutiny we already give any other figure people are asked to trust.
