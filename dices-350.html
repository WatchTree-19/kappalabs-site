Whose safety label? Six AI graders on Google's DICES-350

By Sandeep Singh Rai, Founder

Having spent time with Google Research's DICES-350, I believe credit is due first to the DICES team, for they published with it something most safety benchmarks leave out: every one of its 350 adversarial chatbot conversations was rated by 123 people from a deliberately diverse panel, and separately labelled by trust and safety experts, and the full ratings were released under an open licence. That makes it one of the very few datasets where a simple question can be asked with real evidence behind the answer, being, when an AI grades safety, whose judgment is it matching?

We ran six widely used AI graders over all 350 conversations, three times each: Claude Haiku 4.5, Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5, GPT-4.1 and Gemini 2.5 Flash.

What we found

1. The graders call far fewer conversations unsafe than the experts do. Half the conversations carry an expert "unsafe" label. The six graders call between 6% and 32% unsafe, and catch only 11% to 44% of the conversations the experts flag. The crowd panel's majority sits inside the graders' range, at 23% (the two conversations where the crowd tied are set aside).

2. The two human references often disagree with each other. The crowd majority and the experts give the same verdict on 66% of conversations (95% interval 60% to 70%; ties set aside). Choosing a reference is a real decision, not a formality.

3. Raw agreement flatters graders that rarely say "unsafe". Against the crowd, answering "safe" every time scores 77%, within four points of the best grader. Corrected for that, GPT-4.1, which looked weakest against the crowd, is level with the others there, and against the experts it is the strongest grader on every measure.

"Corrected" is balanced accuracy: the average of the share of unsafe and the share of safe conversations a grader gets right, so answering "safe" every time scores 50%. Half the expert labels are unsafe, so against the experts the two measures coincide.

Why it matters

A sentence such as "our grader agrees with humans 81% of the time" is, I believe, only half a fact until it says which humans, and how often a grader that never flagged anything would agree with them. On DICES, a high raw agreement with the crowd mostly reflects how rarely the crowd says "unsafe", and by the same token, for anyone choosing a safety grader, or relying on a safety score someone else produced, the reference and the baseline behind the number deserve the same scrutiny as the grader itself, since it is on those two choices that the trust placed in a safety score quietly rests.
