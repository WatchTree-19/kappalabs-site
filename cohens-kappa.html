What is Cohen's kappa?

Cohen's kappa (κ) measures how far two raters agree beyond what chance alone would produce. It runs from 1 for perfect agreement, through 0 for agreement no better than chance, to negative values when raters agree less than chance would.

Jacob Cohen introduced it in 1960. It is now the standard way to report whether two people, or a person and an AI judge, give the same labels for reasons other than luck.

The formula

κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe)

Here po is the share of items the two raters agree on, and pe is the agreement expected by chance, worked out from how often each rater uses each label.

A worked example

Two raters label 100 chatbot replies as safe or unsafe. Both say safe on 70, both say unsafe on 10, and they disagree on 20. Each rater calls 20 of the 100 unsafe.

  • Observed agreement: (70 + 10) / 100 = 0.80.
  • Chance agreement: 0.8 × 0.8 + 0.2 × 0.2 = 0.68.
  • Kappa: (0.80 − 0.68) / (1 − 0.68) = 0.375.

So 80% agreement, which sounds strong, is a kappa of about 0.38. Most of that 80% comes from both raters saying "safe" most of the time.

How to read it

KappaCommon reading (Landis and Koch, 1977)
Below 0Worse than chance
0 to 0.20Slight
0.21 to 0.40Fair
0.41 to 0.60Moderate
0.61 to 0.80Substantial
0.81 to 1Almost perfect

These bands are a convention, not a rule. What counts as enough depends on what the labels are used for.

Why raw agreement is not enough

A rater who gives the same answer every time can still score high on raw agreement when one label is common. Its kappa is exactly 0. In our audit of Google's DICES-350, answering "safe" every time matches the crowd 77% of the time. Read the audit

Limits to know

  • It is for two raters and unordered categories. For ordered categories there is weighted kappa.
  • For more than two raters, or missing ratings, use Fleiss' kappa or Krippendorff's alpha.
  • When one label is rare, kappa can be low even with high raw agreement. Report both, with how common each label is.

Why it matters for AI judges

Many AI scores are produced by an AI judge and justified by its agreement with human labels. Kappa asks how much of that agreement is real. Checking it is part of meta-evaluation, and it is where Kappa Labs takes its name.

Common questions

What is a good Cohen's kappa?

By the common Landis and Koch reading, 0.61 to 0.80 is substantial and above 0.80 is almost perfect. These bands are a convention, and what counts as enough depends on what the labels are used for.

What is the difference between Cohen's kappa and percent agreement?

Percent agreement counts how often two raters give the same label. Cohen's kappa subtracts the agreement expected by chance, so a rater who always gives the most common label scores high on percent agreement and 0 on kappa.

Can Cohen's kappa be used for more than two raters?

No. It is defined for two raters. For more raters, or when some ratings are missing, use Fleiss' kappa or Krippendorff's alpha.
